# 3D&T Made Me a Reader, a Coder, and Got Me Actual Friends

<PostDate date={date} />

Before 3D&T, I wasn't much of a reader, and I definitely wasn't the kid who
made friends easily. **Defensores de Tóquio** — 3D&T for short, a Brazilian
tabletop RPG loosely inspired by Dungeon & Dragons — changed both of those
things, and it happened almost by accident.

A friend brought it up one day, and getting into it meant reading rulebooks,
sitting at a table with people I barely knew, and building characters from
scratch. None of that was natural to me at the time. But the game made it
easy to want to, and that "wanting to" did more for me as a teenager than I
realized until much later.

## Learning to read by wanting to know what happens next

I read the 3D&T books the way other kids read comics under the desk — not
because I had to, but because I needed to know what my character could do
next. Rulebooks, supplements, character options: I went through all of it
looking for combinations, backstory hooks, anything that made my next
session better. It's a strange way to become a reader, but it worked. The
motivation wasn't "this is good for you," it was "I need this to play."

## Understanding how stories and worlds actually work

3D&T is where I first understood, hands-on, how world building and system
rules relate to each other. A world needs rules to feel real — how magic
works, how combat resolves, what a character can and can't do — and a story
needs a world to happen in. Sitting at the table, watching a game master
turn a rulebook into a living scene, and then building my own characters
inside that structure, taught me more about narrative mechanics than any
class did. It's a lesson that's followed me into everything I've built
since, including software: systems have rules, and good rules are what let
interesting things emerge.

## Making friends, one campaign at a time

The social part mattered just as much. 3D&T gave me a reason to sit at a
table with other people on a regular basis, working toward something
together instead of just hanging out. Some of my closest friendships as a
teenager started there — not despite the game, but because of it. It gave
awkward teenagers a shared project, which turns out to be a much easier way
to become friends than small talk ever was.

## Fast forward: learning to code, and going straight back to 3D&T

More than ten years later, when I started learning to code, I didn't reach
for a to-do app or a calculator as my first real project — the classic
beginner exercises. I went straight back to 3D&T. Character creation in the
game means tracking characteristics, spending points across Strength,
Skills, Constitution, Armor and Firepower, keeping tabs on life and magic
points, damage types, advantages and disadvantages. Doing all of that by
hand, on paper, session after session, was exactly the kind of repetitive
process I wanted to automate.

That itch became [character-sheet](https://github.com/diegochagas/character-sheet):
a web character sheet and battle simulator for 3D&T. It started years ago as
a CS50 final project in Python and SQL, got rewritten in React once I got
more comfortable with the language, and now runs on Next.js. It handles
character creation with the game's point-spending rules, a full sheet for
characteristics and spells and history, an in-app explanation of the system
itself, and even a 1-vs-1 automated battle simulator that follows the actual
combat rules — initiative, attack and defense force, dodging, critical hits.

It's still up and running at
[character-sheet-eight.vercel.app](https://character-sheet-eight.vercel.app/),
seeded with a roster that includes Goku and Vegeta, because of course it
does.

## The part I haven't solved yet

Here's the part that stings a little: I don't have time to actually play
anymore. Adulthood has a way of eating the exact kind of unstructured,
recurring time that a good campaign needs. The character sheet still works,
the rules are still in my head, but the table I used to sit at doesn't
happen the same way it used to.

I don't have a clean ending for that. Building the tool was its own kind of
tribute to what the game gave me — reading, an understanding of how
systems and stories fit together, and friendships that started over dice
rolls. Maybe that's enough for now. Maybe one day I carve out the time
again. Either way, the code is there, waiting, exactly like a character
sheet is supposed to be.
